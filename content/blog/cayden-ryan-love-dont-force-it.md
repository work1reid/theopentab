For someone who works underground and talks about money in seconds and decades, Cayden Ryan has a surprisingly romantic philosophy about love. Boiled down: don't force it.

## "If love's real, it'll come to you"

That's the whole thesis, and he means it literally:

> "If love's real, it'll come to you. If you try to force it, you're trying to make up something that's not real."

It shapes a stance that's almost contrarian for his generation: he's against dating apps. Not in theory — in practice. He's never downloaded one, never had one, says he never will. His reasoning is consistent with everything else he believes: an app is built around *looking* for someone, and to him that built-in effort is the opposite of real. "It's forced," he says. "It's not real to me."

He's not preachy about it — if it's worked out for you, "good for you, that might be your true love story." It's simply not how he wants his to happen.

## The strange-coincidence story

What gives the philosophy weight is the story behind it. Cayden describes the realest connection he's had — and it arrived through a chain of events he could never have engineered.

He met someone in passing while he was with a different girlfriend, on a trip to a place he happened to be visiting. Numbers were swapped almost incidentally. A year and a half, maybe two, went by with no contact at all. Then one bored night, out of nowhere, a message landed — and from there, it clicked.

"I would have never met her if I wasn't with the girl I was, and if I didn't go away to that place," he says. "I would never have even known she existed."

That's his proof that you can't manufacture the real thing. The realest connections, in his experience, come from sequences too weird and too unplanned to force.

## The takeaway

Strip away the specifics and Cayden's advice is simple, and it applies well beyond dating: stop trying to force outcomes that are meant to arrive on their own. Do the living, stay open, and let the real stuff find you. For a young guy in a world of swipes and metrics, it's a quietly grounding way to look at it.
