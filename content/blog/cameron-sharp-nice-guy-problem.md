One of the most honest threads in Cameron Sharp's conversation isn't about music at all. It's about the quiet cost of being *too* nice.

## The pattern

For years, Cameron played the nice guy. Worried about coming across as arrogant, he'd step back and let other people through — the gigs, the chances, the spotlight. It felt like the decent thing to do.

Then he'd go home feeling flat. "I'd go home feeling pretty sad because I would miss an opportunity." And here's the part that stung: the person he stepped aside for never registered the kindness.

> "That person's not sitting around thinking, 'Cameron's such a nice guy because he gave me an opportunity.' They're thinking, 'look at me, I just succeeded.'"

The generosity was invisible. The missed chance was not.

## The uncomfortable admission

What makes this useful is that Cameron doesn't resolve it into something tidy. He names the genuinely awkward truth:

> "It feels so ugly to admit that you want it more than all these other people. But sometimes you just have to step over that person."

He's still working on the balance — "finding that balance of being a nice person, but also being kind of pushy, being like, no, hang on, this is my opportunity." It doesn't come naturally to him, and he won't pretend it does.

## The line he won't cross

Crucially, this isn't a pivot to ruthlessness. Cameron's clear that ego and rudeness get you nowhere: "You're never going to get anywhere by being rude to people or letting your ego take over." His model is the *quiet achiever* — someone who keeps their head down, stays kind, but stops apologising for wanting it.

It's a distinction worth sitting with, especially for anyone who's ever confused being humble with being passive: you can be a good person and still back yourself. Letting every chance go to someone else isn't humility — it's just a slow way of opting out.
