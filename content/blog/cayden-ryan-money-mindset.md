People throw "millionaire" and "billionaire" around like they're cousins. Cayden Ryan has a way of showing they're not even in the same universe.

## The seconds test

Try it the way he frames it. A *million* seconds — how long is that? It's just over a week. Now a *billion* seconds? Around **33 years**.

> "That just shows you how big the difference is."

A week versus a third of a lifetime. Once you feel that gap, headlines about billionaires read completely differently — and so does your sense of what "a lot of money" actually means. It's the kind of mental model that quietly recalibrates how you think about wealth, ambition, and what's realistically life-changing.

## The answer that reveals the person

Ask most 23-year-olds what they'd do with a fortune and you'll hear about cars and watches. Cayden's instinct goes somewhere else first.

"I'd have enough money where I could retire my parents," he says, "and I'd set all my family and my closest friends up." *Then* — and only then — comes the half-joking bit about a mansion on the beach with his own golf course. If he won the lotto, he says, he'd give a lot of it away.

It's a tell. The dream isn't the stuff; it's lifting the people around him.

## He also knows money's dark side

What keeps this from being naïve is how clear-eyed he is about the downside. Cayden reels off cautionary tales of lottery winners whose lives came apart, and lands on a sharp observation: the danger usually isn't the winner.

"Money changes people," he says — "not the people winning the lotto, but the people around it."

That's an unusually mature read for someone his age: that sudden wealth tests everyone in your orbit, and that the threat often comes from outside, not within. Combine the seconds analogy with the "family first" instinct and the wariness about what money does to people, and you get a money mindset most adults could learn from.
